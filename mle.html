1. Why we need this: where do loss functions come from?

So far each model came with a loss that was just handed to you. Linear regression used squared error. Logistic regression used cross-entropy, justified by "it's convex and the gradient is clean". Why those? Would $|y - \hat y|^3$ be just as good?

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the principle behind those choices. It goes in two steps:

  1. Say what you believe about how the data was generated, as a probability distribution with unknown parameters $\theta$: "each flip is heads with probability $\phi$", "grades are bell-shaped around some $\mu$".
  2. Choose the $\theta$ under which the data you actually observed is most probable.

Do that and the loss isn't a choice any more. It's derived. By the end of this chapter, cross-entropy will come out of a coin-flip model with no extra assumptions (§8).

Analogy — the detective

You find a coin that came up heads in 9 of 10 flips. Suspects: "fair coin", "coin with $\phi = 0.9$", "coin with $\phi = 0.5$ but you got lucky". The detective asks of each suspect: if it were you, how likely is what we saw? The suspect that makes the evidence least surprising is the best explanation. MLE is that detective, searching over every possible $\phi$. (Where it breaks: a real detective also weighs how plausible each suspect was beforehand. MLE doesn't — that's the extra step in MAP estimation, later in the course.)

Common mistake

"A density can't be bigger than 1." It can. A normal with $\sigma = 0.1$ has a peak density of $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,0.1} \approx 3.99$. Only the area must be 1. So a Gaussian likelihood can be larger than 1, and its log can be positive, and neither means something went wrong.

3. Likelihood: how probable is the data, given the parameters?

Likelihood

For a dataset $\D = \{y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(N)}\}$ and parameters $\theta$, the likelihood is the probability (or density) of the data given the parameters:

$$ p(\D\mid\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} p\bigl(y^{(i)}\mid\theta\bigr). $$

The superscript $(i)$ is an index ("the $i$-th data point"), not a power.

Why can we multiply? Because we assume the data is i.i.d.: independent (one flip doesn't affect the next) and identically distributed (every flip uses the same $\theta$). For independent events, the joint probability is the product. Without that assumption you'd be stuck with each outcome depending on all the previous ones, each with its own parameters:

$$ p(y^{(1)}\mid\theta_1)\,p(y^{(2)}\mid y^{(1)},\theta_2)\,p(y^{(3)}\mid y^{(1)},y^{(2)},\theta_3)\cdots $$

which you couldn't estimate from one dataset.

$\mu = 80$, $\sigma = 5$

Takeaway

The second is about 6× more likely. The narrow curve at 80 almost rules out the 95 (density 0.000886), and that one point drags its whole product down. Notice also how small these numbers are with only three points. That becomes a real problem in §5.

4. Maximum likelihood: flip the question around

A single likelihood value like $3.42\times10^{-6}$ means almost nothing on its own. Comparing two values does: it told us $(85, 10^2)$ beats $(80, 5^2)$. MLE takes that to its conclusion and searches over all parameter values for the best one:

$$ \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \argmax_\theta\ p(\D\mid\theta) = \argmax_\theta \prod_{i=1}^N p\bigl(y^{(i)}\mid\theta\bigr). $$

To stress that we're now varying $\theta$ with the data held fixed, it's written as the likelihood function $L(\theta;\D) = p(\D\mid\theta)$, often just $L(\theta)$. It is the same formula as before. The only thing that changed is which symbol we treat as the variable.

Figure 2 — the coin's likelihood function $L(\phi) = \phi^{N_H}(1-\phi)^{N_T}$
Starts at the notes' dataset $\{1,1,0,1\}$. The dot is the maximum, found here by checking 2,000 grid points. What to notice: (1) the peak is always at $N_H/(N_H+N_T)$, which §6 derives. (2) Multiply both counts by 10 (30 heads, 10 tails) and the peak stays put but gets much narrower: more data, more certainty. (3) The readout's "area under $L$" is not 1 ($\tfrac{1}{20}$ for the starting data). The likelihood is not a probability distribution over $\phi$. (4) Tick "log": the curve changes shape but the peak doesn't move.
Common mistake: $p(\D\mid\theta)$ is not $p(\theta\mid\D)$

It's tempting to read "the likelihood of $\phi = 0.75$ is 0.105" as "there's a 10.5% chance $\phi$ is 0.75". It isn't. The likelihood is still the probability of the data given the parameters. We're just looking for the parameters that make it largest. Figure 2's area readout makes it concrete: integrate $L$ over $\phi$ and you don't get 1. Turning it into $p(\theta\mid\D)$ needs Bayes' rule and a prior.

5. Take the log, then negate it

In practice we never maximise $L$ directly. We use the log-likelihood

$$ \ell(\theta;\D) = \log L(\theta;\D) = \log\prod_{i=1}^N p\bigl(y^{(i)}\mid\theta\bigr) = \sum_{i=1}^N \log p\bigl(y^{(i)}\mid\theta\bigr), $$

for two reasons:

Reason 1 — underflow

A product of many numbers below 1 shrinks exponentially. Three grades already gave $10^{-6}$. With thousands of points, the true value is smaller than the smallest number a computer can store, so it gets rounded to exactly 0. Then every $\theta$ "has likelihood 0" and you can't compare them. A sum of logs stays a reasonable size.

Reason 2 — easier calculus

Differentiating a product of $N$ factors needs the product rule $N$ times. The log turns it into a sum, which you differentiate term by term. It also cancels the $\exp$ in Gaussians (and other exponential-family distributions).

Figure 3 — break the computer: multiply 0.5 by itself $N$ times
The likelihood of any particular sequence of $N$ fair-coin flips is $0.5^N$. The page computes it two ways in double precision: a running product, and a sum of logs. What to notice: drag past $N = 1074$. At $N = 1075$ the running product becomes exactly 0 (the smallest storable positive double is $2^{-1074}$), and the pink line drops off the chart. The log version keeps going as a straight line with $\log_{10}$-likelihood $-N\log_{10}2$. Real datasets have millions of points.

Why is it allowed? $\log$ is monotonically increasing: if $L(\theta_B) > L(\theta_A)$ then $\log L(\theta_B) > \log L(\theta_A)$. It changes the values but never the order, so the $\theta$ at the top is the same:

$$ \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \argmax_\theta\ p(\D\mid\theta) = \argmax_\theta\ \log p(\D\mid\theta). $$

Then negate. We've built all our tools around minimising (gradient descent), so flip the sign: $\argmax_\theta \ell(\theta) = \argmin_\theta\,[-\ell(\theta)]$. The negative log-likelihood (NLL) is now an ordinary loss to minimise.

The MLE recipe
  1. Write down the likelihood $p(\D\mid\theta)$.
  2. Set the objective to the negative log-likelihood: $J(\theta) = -\log p(\D\mid\theta)$.
  3. Compute $\partial J/\partial\theta$.
  4. Find $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}$ by (a) setting the derivative to zero and solving, or (b) gradient descent.

Steps 3 and 4 are exactly what we've done for linear regression, logistic regression, and neural networks. Only steps 1–2 are new.

6. MLE for a coin: the fraction of heads

A notation trick first. With specific data we could write each factor as $\phi$ or $1-\phi$ by inspection. With a general $y^{(i)}$ we need one expression that picks the right one automatically. Use the indicator $\mathbb 1(\cdot)$, which is 1 when its condition is true and 0 otherwise, as an exponent:

$$ p\bigl(y^{(i)}\mid\phi\bigr) = \phi^{\,\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=1)}\,(1-\phi)^{\,\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=0)}. $$

If $y^{(i)} = 1$ this is $\phi^1(1-\phi)^0 = \phi$. If $y^{(i)} = 0$ it's $\phi^0(1-\phi)^1 = 1-\phi$. Anything to the power 0 is 1, so the factor that doesn't apply drops out.

Derivation

Suppose $\D$ has $N_H$ heads and $N_T$ tails ($N = N_H + N_T$).

Derivation

Log-likelihood. The log of a product is the sum of logs, and $\log a^b = b\log a$:

$$ \ell(\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^N\Bigl[\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=1)\log\phi + \mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=0)\log(1-\phi)\Bigr] = N_H\log\phi + N_T\log(1-\phi). $$

(Summing the indicator $\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=1)$ over all $i$ just counts the heads.)

Differentiate and set to zero.

$$ \frac{d\ell}{d\phi} = \frac{N_H}{\phi} - \frac{N_T}{1-\phi} = \frac{(1-\phi)N_H - \phi N_T}{\phi(1-\phi)} = 0. $$

A fraction is zero when its numerator is: $(1-\phi)N_H - \phi N_T = 0 \Rightarrow N_H - \phi(N_H + N_T) = 0$, so

$$ \hat\phi_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{N_H}{N_H + N_T} = \frac{N_H}{N}. $$

Is it a maximum? $\frac{d^2\ell}{d\phi^2} = -\frac{N_H}{\phi^2} - \frac{N_T}{(1-\phi)^2} < 0$ everywhere, so $\ell$ is concave and this is the maximum. (The notes skip this check.)

That's the answer you'd have guessed, the fraction of flips that came up heads. MLE agrees with intuition, and the same machinery keeps working when intuition runs out.

Now the warning: after one flip that lands heads, the MLE is $\hat\phi = 1$, "this coin always lands heads". With little data, MLE trusts the data completely and overfits. That's the same disease regularisation treated in chapter 12, and MAP estimation will treat it here.

7. MLE for a die: the trick the notes held back

The same indicator trick, with one-hot data. For data point $i$, exactly one $y_k^{(i)}$ is 1:

$$ p\bigl(\mathbf y^{(i)}\mid\boldsymbol\phi\bigr) = \prod_{k=1}^{K}\phi_k^{\,\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}_k = 1)}, \qquad L(\boldsymbol\phi) = \prod_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\phi_k^{\,\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}_k = 1)}. $$

For "green" $= [0,1,0,0]^\top$, the inner product is $\phi_1^0\phi_2^1\phi_3^0\phi_4^0 = \phi_2$. Every non-matching side contributes a factor of 1. Taking logs and swapping the order of the two sums:

$$ \ell(\boldsymbol\phi) = \sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{k=1}^K \mathbb 1(y_k^{(i)} = 1)\log\phi_k = \sum_{k=1}^K\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbb 1(y_k^{(i)} = 1)\Bigr]\log\phi_k = \sum_{k=1}^K N_k\log\phi_k, $$

where $N_k$ is how many times side $k$ came up.

The notes stop here: "we're going to hold off… as it involves one more trick related to the fact that $\sum_k\phi_k = 1$". Here's that trick.

Solving it (constrained maximisation)

Why the usual move fails. Set $\partial\ell/\partial\phi_k = N_k/\phi_k = 0$? That has no solution. $\ell$ just keeps rising as every $\phi_k$ grows. Without the constraint, "make every side more likely" always helps. But the $\phi_k$ must sum to 1: raising one forces another down.

Lagrange multiplier. To maximise $\ell$ subject to $\sum_k\phi_k = 1$, maximise $\ell(\boldsymbol\phi) - \lambda\bigl(\sum_k\phi_k - 1\bigr)$ over $\boldsymbol\phi$, where $\lambda$ is a new unknown:

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_k}: \quad \frac{N_k}{\phi_k} - \lambda = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \phi_k = \frac{N_k}{\lambda}. $$

Pin down $\lambda$ using the constraint: $\sum_k\phi_k = \sum_k N_k/\lambda = N/\lambda = 1$, so $\lambda = N$. Therefore

$$ \hat\phi_{k,\text{MLE}} = \frac{N_k}{N}. $$

Intuition for $\lambda$: at the optimum, the marginal gain from moving a little probability onto side $k$, which is $N_k/\phi_k$, must be the same for every side. If it weren't, you'd shift probability from the side with the smaller gain to the one with the larger gain and do better. $\lambda$ is that common value.

No-Lagrange check: with $K = 2$, substitute $\phi_2 = 1 - \phi_1$. You get $N_1\log\phi_1 + N_2\log(1-\phi_1)$, which is exactly the coin from §6, with answer $N_1/N$. ✓

8. MLE for a Gaussian: the mean, and the variance

Data $x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(N)}$, model $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$. Log of each density:

$$ \log\Bigl[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}e^{-\frac{(x^{(i)}-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\Bigr] = -\log\sqrt{2\pi} - \log\sigma - \frac{(x^{(i)}-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}, $$

so summing over $i$,

$$ \ell(\mu,\sigma) = -N\log\sqrt{2\pi} - N\log\sigma - \sum_{i=1}^N\frac{(x^{(i)}-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}. $$

The log has removed the exponential, as promised.

The mean

Only the last term involves $\mu$:

$$ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu} = \sum_{i=1}^N\frac{2(x^{(i)}-\mu)}{2\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^N(x^{(i)} - \mu) = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \sum_i x^{(i)} = N\mu \ \Longrightarrow\ \hat\mu = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N x^{(i)}. $$

The sample average, as you'd expect. Notice that $\sigma$ dropped out, so $\hat\mu$ is the same whatever the variance is.

The variance (not in the notes)

Differentiate with respect to $\sigma$. The first term is constant, $\frac{d}{d\sigma}(-N\log\sigma) = -\frac N\sigma$, and $\frac{d}{d\sigma}\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} = -\frac{1}{\sigma^3}$:

$$ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\sigma} = -\frac{N}{\sigma} + \frac{1}{\sigma^3}\sum_{i=1}^N(x^{(i)}-\mu)^2 = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \hat\sigma^2 = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\bigl(x^{(i)} - \hat\mu\bigr)^2. $$

The average squared distance from the mean. The two terms pull in opposite directions. $-N\log\sigma$ rewards a narrow curve (tall peak, high density). The squared-error term punishes a curve too narrow to reach the data. The MLE balances them.

The "sample variance" in statistics divides by $N-1$ to correct this (108.33 instead of 72.22 for the grades). MLE gives the $N$ version.

9. The payoff: logistic regression is MLE

So far we've modelled one variable on its own. For prediction we want the distribution of the label given the input, $p(y\mid\mathbf x,\theta)$. The recipe: collect i.i.d. pairs $(\mathbf x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})$, choose a form for $p(y\mid\mathbf x,\theta)$, and maximise

$$ L(\theta) = p\bigl(y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(N)}\mid\mathbf x^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf x^{(N)},\theta\bigr) = \prod_{i=1}^N p\bigl(y^{(i)}\mid\mathbf x^{(i)},\theta\bigr). $$
Conditional likelihood (and why nobody says "MCLE")

This is the conditional likelihood: we condition on the inputs and only model the labels. Maximising it is strictly "maximum conditional likelihood estimation". In ML it's used so often that people drop the "conditional" and call it the likelihood and MLE. Some even write it $p(\D\mid\theta)$, which is confusing because $\D$ contains the $\mathbf x$'s on the right of the bar. Read it as conditional.

Binary

Logistic regression says $p(y = 1\mid\mathbf x) = \sigma(\theta^\top\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-\theta^\top\mathbf x}}$. Write $\phi^{(i)} = \sigma(\theta^\top\mathbf x^{(i)})$. Each label is now a coin flip whose bias depends on its input. So the likelihood is the Bernoulli one from §6, with a different $\phi$ per point:

$$ L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^N\bigl(\phi^{(i)}\bigr)^{\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=1)}\bigl(1-\phi^{(i)}\bigr)^{\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=0)}, \qquad -\ell(\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^N\Bigl[\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=1)\log\phi^{(i)} + \mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=0)\log(1-\phi^{(i)})\Bigr]. $$

Now make three substitutions, each true by definition: $\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=1) = y^{(i)}$ (since $y\in\{0,1\}$), $\mathbb 1(y^{(i)}=0) = 1 - y^{(i)}$, and $\phi^{(i)} = \hat y^{(i)}$:

$$ \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \argmin_\theta\ -\sum_{i=1}^N\Bigl[y^{(i)}\log\hat y^{(i)} + \bigl(1-y^{(i)}\bigr)\log\bigl(1-\hat y^{(i)}\bigr)\Bigr]. $$
Key idea

That's exactly the binary cross-entropy objective from chapter 11, up to the constant factor $\frac1N$, which doesn't move the argmin. Cross-entropy was not an arbitrary choice. It is the NLL of a Bernoulli model.

Multi-class

With softmax, $\phi_k^{(i)} = \frac{\exp(\theta_k^\top\mathbf x^{(i)})}{\sum_{l=1}^K\exp(\theta_l^\top\mathbf x^{(i)})}$: each label is a die roll whose weights depend on the input. That gives the categorical likelihood from §7:

$$ -\ell(\Theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{k=1}^K\mathbb 1(y_k^{(i)}=1)\log\phi_k^{(i)} = -\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{k=1}^K y_k^{(i)}\log\hat y_k^{(i)}, $$

which is the multi-class cross-entropy, again.

And linear regression? going deeper

Assume $y = \theta^\top\mathbf x + \varepsilon$ with noise $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$, so $p(y\mid\mathbf x) = \mathcal N(\theta^\top\mathbf x,\sigma^2)$. Then

$$ -\log p\bigl(y^{(i)}\mid\mathbf x^{(i)}\bigr) = \frac{\bigl(y^{(i)} - \theta^\top\mathbf x^{(i)}\bigr)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \log\bigl(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma\bigr). $$

The second term doesn't involve $\theta$ and $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}$ is a positive constant, so minimising the NLL over $\theta$ is minimising the sum of squared errors. Squared error is the NLL of Gaussian noise.

10. Recap

  1. Likelihood $p(\D\mid\theta) = \prod_i p(y^{(i)}\mid\theta)$ under i.i.d. As a function of $\theta$ it's $L(\theta)$. It is not a distribution over $\theta$.
  2. MLE: $\hat\theta = \argmax_\theta L(\theta) = \argmax_\theta \ell(\theta) = \argmin_\theta -\ell(\theta)$. Logs avoid underflow and simplify the calculus, and they're safe because log is monotonic.
  3. Recipe: likelihood → NLL → derivative → set to 0 or run gradient descent.
  4. Indicator-exponent trick: $\phi^{\mathbb 1(y=1)}(1-\phi)^{\mathbb 1(y=0)}$ and $\prod_k\phi_k^{\mathbb 1(y_k=1)}$.
  5. Results: Bernoulli $\hat\phi = N_H/N$; categorical $\hat\phi_k = N_k/N$ (Lagrange multiplier, $\lambda = N$); Gaussian $\hat\mu = $ mean and $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1N\sum(x-\hat\mu)^2$.
  6. Conditional MLE with a Bernoulli/categorical label model is binary/multi-class cross-entropy. With Gaussian noise it's squared error.
  7. MLE overfits with little data: one heads gives $\hat\phi = 1$, and an unseen class gets probability 0.

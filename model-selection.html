Part II · Chapter 12

Model selection: regularization & cross-validation

ERM always prefers the more complex model. This chapter gives you two tools that push back — a penalty on complexity inside the objective, and held-out data outside it — and the recipe for choosing any hyperparameter honestly.

1. Where we are: polynomial features, and the question they leave open

The lecture opens with data that is plainly curved ($\mathcal X = \R$, $\mathcal Y = \R$). A line $y = wx + b$ won't fit it, but a quadratic $h(x) = \theta_2 x^2 + \theta_1 x + \theta_0$ will. Generalise: $h(x) = \theta_k x^k + \dots + \theta_1 x + \theta_0$, or more abstractly, any feature map $\phi : \mathcal X \to \R^{d'}$. The recipe, which you already know from chapter 10:

  1. Transform the training set: $(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}) \mapsto (\phi(x^{(i)}), y^{(i)})$.
  2. Train an ordinary linear regressor $h : \R^{d'} \to \R$ on the transformed data.
  3. Predict on a new point with $h(\phi(x^{\text{new}}))$.

Here $d'$ is the number of features after the transform — for a degree-$k$ polynomial, $\phi(x) = [x, x^2, \dots, x^k]$ plus the bias.

The open question: how do you choose $k$? The tempting answer is "use ERM — pick the $k$ with the lowest training error". That answer is wrong, and it's wrong in a predictable direction.

Key idea — why ERM can't choose $k$

Every degree-$k$ polynomial is also a degree-$(k+1)$ polynomial (set $\theta_{k+1} = 0$). So the degree-$(k+1)$ model class contains the degree-$k$ class, and its best training error can only be equal or lower. ERM therefore always prefers higher $k$. Higher-degree polynomials bend to fit the noise in the data, not just the signal: overfitting.

The lecture lists three responses. Get more data — effective, but often expensive. The other two are this chapter: regularization (change the objective) and cross-validation (change how you evaluate).

2. Regularization: charge the model for complexity

Analogy

An expense policy. Without one, an employee (ERM) will buy the most expensive tool for every job, because it's always at least as good. With a policy that charges them for each purchase, they only buy the expensive tool when it's enough better to justify the price. $\lambda$ is the price. Where the analogy leaks: the "price" here isn't money but a penalty in the same units as the loss, and you set its size by experiment (§5), not by accounting.

Definition — regularized ERM $$ \theta^\star \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta\in\R^{d'}} \;\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(\theta^\top\phi(x^{(i)}) - y^{(i)}\Bigr)^2}_{\text{empirical risk}} \;+\; \lambda\,\underbrace{r(\theta)}_{\text{regularizer}} $$
  • $r : \R^{d'} \to \R$ is the regularizer: a number measuring how "complex" $\theta$ is. More complex → bigger.
  • $\lambda > 0$ is the regularization strength: how much weight the complexity penalty gets relative to the empirical risk.

Why this works, in the lecture's words: a more complex model always has lower empirical risk because it can fit more complex data. The regularizer is larger for more complex models, so now a complex model is only chosen if its drop in empirical risk is big enough to pay for its increase in $\lambda r(\theta)$. Otherwise a simpler model wins.

Try itEvaluate the three penalties

For $\theta = [3,\ 0,\ -4]$ (bias already excluded), compute the $L_0$, LASSO and ridge penalties. Then: which penalty would be unchanged if the first entry went from $3$ to $0.001$?

Step 1 — Compute each

$L_0$: two entries are non-zero → $2$. LASSO: $|3| + |0| + |-4| = 7$. Ridge: $9 + 0 + 16 = 25$.

Answer & the takeaway

$L_0$ stays $2$ — it only cares whether an entry is non-zero, not how big. That's exactly why it's hard to optimize: shrinking a coefficient gradually gains nothing until it hits exactly $0$, so there's no gradient to follow. LASSO drops to $4.001$ and ridge to about $16$ — both reward shrinking, which is what makes them tractable.

Why LASSO gives exact zeros and ridge doesn't

The notes state "LASSO also encourages sparsity" without saying why. The picture that explains it: minimizing loss plus $\lambda r(\theta)$ is equivalent to minimizing the loss subject to $r(\theta) \le t$ for some budget $t$. The loss has elliptical contours around its unregularized minimum; you want the lowest contour that touches the allowed region.

Figure 1 — the $L_1$ diamond has corners; the $L_2$ disc doesn't
What to notice: at the starting position the LASSO solution sits exactly on the corner $(1, 0)$ — $\theta_2$ is zeroed — while ridge keeps a small non-zero $\theta_2$. Move the optimum around: for a whole range of positions the diamond's solution stays pinned to a corner; the disc's solution lands exactly on an axis only for special, knife-edge positions. Drag $\theta_2$ up to about $0.7$ and LASSO leaves the corner too — sparsity is likely, not guaranteed. The constrained optimum is found numerically by scanning the boundary.

Practical rules from the lecture

Common mistake 1 — penalizing the bias

Exclude $\theta_0$ from the regularizer. The bias just shifts predictions up or down to match the average $y$. Penalizing it drags every prediction toward $0$ — if all your targets are around $1000$, a penalized bias would systematically under-predict, which has nothing to do with complexity.

Common mistake 2 — mixing scales

Standardize features first: same units, same scale — subtract each feature's training mean and divide by its spread. The penalty treats every $\theta_j$ the same, but a feature measured in millimetres needs a coefficient 1000× larger than the same feature in metres, so it would get penalized far more for no reason. (The notes say "divide by the variance"; the standard choice is the standard deviation, which puts every feature on unit scale. Dividing by the variance would leave a feature with spread $\sigma$ at scale $1/\sigma$, not $1$.)

Other regularizers exist that the lecture mentions but doesn't cover: dropout and early stopping (you'll meet both with neural networks). And the obvious question — how do you choose $\lambda$? — is §4–5.

Guided problemRidge by hand, one parameter

Data: $(x, y) = (1, 2),\ (2, 4),\ (3, 7)$. Model $h(x) = \theta x$ (no bias, no features). Objective

$$ J(\theta) = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}(\theta x^{(i)} - y^{(i)})^2 + \lambda\theta^2 . $$

Find $\theta^\star$ as a function of $\lambda$, then evaluate at $\lambda = 0, 0.5, 1, 5$ together with the training MSE.

Step 1 — Differentiate and set to zero
$$ J'(\theta) = \frac{2}{N}\sum_i x^{(i)}(\theta x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}) + 2\lambda\theta = 0 $$ $$ \Longrightarrow\quad \theta^\star = \frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i x^{(i)}y^{(i)}}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i (x^{(i)})^2 + \lambda}. $$

$J$ is a parabola in $\theta$ opening upward (the $\theta^2$ coefficient is $\frac1N\sum x^2 + \lambda > 0$), so this is the minimum.

Step 2 — Plug in the data

$\sum xy = 2 + 8 + 21 = 31$, $\sum x^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$, $N = 3$:

$$ \theta^\star(\lambda) = \frac{31/3}{14/3 + \lambda}. $$

4. Hyperparameters: the things ERM can't choose

The lecture returns to decision trees for a second example of the same problem.

Figure 3 (reconstructed) — split again for one outlier, or stop?
Reconstructed from the notes' figure, which lost its points in extraction; only the split values $x_1 = 3.5$ and $x_2 = 4.5$ survived. The layout of the points is illustrative.

Should the tree keep splitting until the lone point in the top right is classified correctly? For: it may contain real information. Against: its label may just be noise. Training error can't settle this — the extra split always lowers it.

Definition

A hyperparameter is a choice about the model class or training procedure that ERM itself is biased on — it would always choose the more complex option. The lecture's examples: tree depth, $\lambda$, and polynomial degree $k$. (Learning rate and number of neural-network layers are hyperparameters too.)

The lecture's pivot: what if we had some more labelled data? Evaluate both trees on that other data and pick the one with lower error. Data the model wasn't trained on can't be overfitted by that model — that's the whole idea of cross-validation.

5. Cross-validation

Holdout cross-validation

Algorithm — holdout CV
  1. Randomly split the training samples into a training set and a validation set (typically 80 : 20).
  2. For each candidate value of the hyperparameter(s): train on the training set; compute the average error on the validation set.
  3. Choose the value with the lowest validation error.
  4. Retrain the model with the chosen value on the entire data.

Problem: with few samples, the validation set is tiny, and the score depends heavily on which points happened to land in it. Re-split and you might choose a different $k$. That's high variance.

$k$-fold cross-validation

Algorithm — $k$-fold CV

Randomly partition the training data into $k$ equal-sized folds $F_1, \dots, F_k$. For each candidate hyperparameter value:

  1. For each fold $i = 1, \dots, k$: train on the other $k-1$ folds $\bigcup_{j\ne i}F_j$; compute the error $\text{error}_i$ on $F_i$.
  2. Cross-validation error $= \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\text{error}_i$.

Choose the value with the lowest CV error, then retrain on the full dataset with it.

Every point is used for validation exactly once, and the $k$ estimates are averaged, so the choice is far less at the mercy of one lucky split. Common choices: $k = 5$ or $k = 10$. The extreme $k = N$ — each fold is a single point — is leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV).

Try itHow many models do you train?

$N = 100$ samples; you're choosing $\lambda$ from 4 candidate values. How many times do you train a model with (a) 5-fold CV, (b) LOOCV? Include the final retrain.

Step 1 — Count per candidate

$k$-fold trains once per fold per candidate: $4 \times k$.

Answer & the takeaway

(a) $4 \times 5 + 1 = 21$. (b) $4 \times 100 + 1 = 401$. LOOCV uses the data most thoroughly but costs $N$ trainings per candidate — fine for linear regression, painful for a large neural network. That cost is why $5$ and $10$ are the defaults.

After 5-fold CV picks $\lambda = 0.1$, what do you deploy?
Why: CV chooses the hyperparameter, not the parameters. The fold models were each trained on only 80% of the data; once $\lambda$ is chosen you retrain on 100%. Picking the best fold (A) just rewards the luckiest split.
You tune $\lambda$ with CV, then report the CV error of the chosen $\lambda$ as your model's expected error on new data. What's wrong?
Why: choosing the minimum of several noisy estimates selects in favour of a lucky one. beyond syllabus The clean fix is a separate test set that is never touched during model selection — or nested CV.

6. Feature engineering vs feature learning

The lecture ends with a distinction it will revisit — and which is the bridge to the next chapter.

Feature engineeringFeature learning
Humans decide what features may be useful ($x^2$, $\log x$, …)Useful features / representations are discovered automatically from data
Humans implement the feature-extraction algorithmTypically needs large datasets
Higher interpretability—

Everything so far in this course has been feature engineering: you chose $\phi$. A neural network learns its own $\phi$ — its hidden layers are a feature map with trainable parameters — which is exactly why it needs more data and is harder to interpret.

7. Recap

  1. Richer model classes ($\phi$, higher degree, deeper trees) always win on training error, so ERM alone overfits.
  2. Regularized ERM: $\arg\min_\theta \frac1N\sum_i(\theta^\top\phi(x^{(i)}) - y^{(i)})^2 + \lambda r(\theta)$. A complex model must lower the risk by more than its penalty.
  3. $L_0$ (count non-zeros) — sparse but hard to optimize; LASSO $\sum|\theta_j|$ — sparse, tractable; ridge $\sum\theta_j^2$ — shrinks, rarely zeros.
  4. Don't penalize the bias; standardize features first.
  5. Hyperparameters (depth, $\lambda$, degree) are chosen on held-out data, not training data.
  6. Holdout (80:20) is simple but high-variance with little data; $k$-fold ($k = 5, 10$) averages $k$ estimates; LOOCV is $k = N$. Always retrain on all data with the chosen value.
  7. Feature engineering: humans choose features, interpretable. Feature learning: learned from (lots of) data.
